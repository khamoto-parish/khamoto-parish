PART 1 • WHO WE ARE

Where is Khamoto?


Khamoto is a parish in Bungokho Central, Mbale District, in the Bugisu region of Eastern Uganda.


The people here are mainly farmers. Rice from the wetlands is their main livelihood, and land is not just property - it is identity, family, and survival.


"Land here is not just property - it is identity, family, and school fees for children."

PART 2D • CULTURE OF THE COMMUNITY

Culture - Because We Live Together, We Have Become One


Because all these tribes are in the area, they have become one culture:


a) Intermarriage


Bagwere marry Bagisu, Bagisu marry Banyole, Banyole marry Bagwere, and so on. Families are now mixed. Khamoto is one family now, not separate tribes.


b) Born In The Area


Many people in Khamoto were born in Khamoto itself. Even if their grandparents came from elsewhere, they themselves are children of Khamoto. Khamoto is their home and birthplace.


c) Circumcision


They all practice circumcision. Even tribes that did not circumcise before have now adopted circumcision as part of their culture because they live together with Bagisu. Circumcision / Imbalu is now a shared culture for all in Khamoto.






PART 3 • HOW WE LIVE - FULL STORY

🌾 Agriculture & Livelihood in Khamoto


In Khamoto, the community grows most crops and rears animals. Farming is not a business here - it is life. From sunrise, families are in the gardens, in the wetlands, and with their animals. Everything a family eats, everything they sell, and everything they pay school fees with comes from the soil.




Mostly SubsistenceGrow for Home + Small Surplus to Sell


Manual LabourMost Work is by Hand - Hoe, Panga, Hands


Few MechanisedOnly a Few Can Afford Machines / Ox-plough




What Do We Grow?


Rice is king in Khamoto. The wetlands around Khamoto, Tumbu and Nakhalapa give the best rice in Bungokho. Families plant rice in the wet season, transplant it by hand, weed it, and harvest it together. When rice does well, children go to school. When it fails, the whole parish feels it.


But rice is not alone. Families also grow maize, beans, cassava, sweet potatoes, groundnuts, millet, and vegetables like cabbages, tomatoes, onions and nakati. Cassava and sweet potatoes are the food security crops - when rice money finishes, these keep families alive. Beans give protein and quick cash. Maize is for posho at home and for selling at the trading centre.


Animals We Rear


Almost every home keeps some animals. Cattle, goats, sheep, pigs, and poultry - chicken, ducks and turkeys - are kept in small numbers. Animals are like a bank: a goat can be sold for emergency medicine, a cow for school fees, a chicken for salt and soap. But because land is small, there is no free range. Animals are tethered, zero-grazed, or kept in small kraals near home.


Limited Land - Fragmented Plots


The area has limited land for farming. Because the population is growing fast, land is now fragmented into small plots - one family may own many small pieces in different places: one piece in the wetland for rice, one small piece on the hill for cassava, another piece near home for vegetables. Walking from one garden to another takes time, and it makes farming harder and more expensive.


Land fragmentation also means families cannot expand. A father divides his small land among 4 or 5 sons, and each son gets even smaller land. That is why land disputes in Khamoto are so painful - there is no extra land to go to.


How We Work - Labour For Money


Farming is the main source of income for the community. It pays for food, school fees, medicine, and family needs. But not everyone has land.


For The Poor: Those who are poor and have little or no land tend to work and offer labour for money - they dig, plant, weed, transplant rice, and harvest in other people's gardens to earn a living. In planting season, whole groups move from garden to garden, working for 10,000 to 20,000 shillings a day. This labour is the survival of many families, especially widows, youth, and landless families.


Women do most of the farming work in Khamoto - planting, weeding, harvesting, drying rice, and carrying it home - while also cooking, fetching water and caring for children. Youth help after school and during holidays. Elders advise on seasons, when to plant, and which seed to use.


Challenges Farmers Face


Farmers in Khamoto face many problems: unpredictable rain, floods that wash away rice, drought that dries wetlands, expensive seeds and fertiliser, lack of machines, poor roads to market, and low prices when middlemen cheat them. Many farmers sell rice immediately after harvest when price is low, because they need money urgently for school fees. If they could store and wait, they would earn more.


Despite all this, Khamoto farmers are hardworking, resilient and proud. They feed their families from their own hands.

PART 5 • THE AMOS INCIDENT

The 70-Acre Land Dispute That Divided A Community


For over 12 years, from 2009 to 2021, Khamoto Village was torn apart by a dispute over 70 acres of land - and at the centre of it was one family fighting to keep what they believed was theirs.


Mubbala Amos and his brothers belong to the family of Tegule Difasi (Balalaka Abengoma clan). The brothers are: Wafenya Dauson, Lodda Davis, Waita Edward, Wairagala James, and the late Gasiwo Amulamu.


In 1983, this family of Tegule Difasi bought the land from that of Dongo - from the family of James Tatukayo, known as Dongo. They bought it as one family, one blood, one claim.


Mubbala Amos was the main plaintiff. He went to court as the main plaintiff for himself and for all his brothers, for their children, and for their future. He was not speaking for himself alone - he was speaking for the whole family of Tegule Difasi.


Amos did not fight alone. He stood together with his brothers. His brother Wafenya Dauson stood beside him through meetings, mediations and court. His brother Gasiwo Amulamu, who has since passed away, also stood for the family until his death. Lodda Davis, Wairagala James, and Waita Edward - all brothers - continued the same struggle, sharing the burden, the costs, and the fear together.


On the other side was James Tatukayo, known as Dongo, who later claimed the land belonged to him through ancestral ties. He said his forefathers had lived on that land, and that no sale could erase that history.


The dispute quickly grew bigger than two families. Local politicians and many community members backed Dongo, while others stood with Amos and his brothers. What started as a boundary issue became a test of who truly belonged in Bugisu - and neighbours who had once farmed side by side stopped trusting each other.


For 12 years, the family lived with eviction threats, court dates, and fear. Every planting season was uncertain - would they harvest, or would they be chased? But the brothers stayed together, and they refused to give up the land they believed their family had bought for them.

PART 6 • WHY IT GREW BIGGER

Why It Became An Identity Conflict


The tension went deeper than land. The Mubbala family are Bagwere from Pallisa, part of the Balalaka Abengoma clan, family of Tegule Difasi. Some people in Khamoto argued that outsiders should not acquire large pieces of land in Bugisu.


During the old boundary conflicts between Bagwere and Bagisu, some community members accused the Balalaka of not supporting Bugisu, and even of passing intelligence to the other side. Whether true or not, those accusations created suspicion, fear, and hatred that lasted for years.


The community lived in insecurity. Neighbours stopped trusting each other. The land dispute became a symbol of a bigger question: Who has the right to own land in Bugisu?






PART 7 • RECONCILIATION

Peace In 2021


After years of eviction threats, mediation, and fear, the story took a surprising turn.


2021: Dongo decided to step down and hand over the land to Amos and his brothers, saying the land had been grabbed from them. It was a rare moment of reconciliation that brought calm back to Khamoto after more than a decade of tension.






PART 8 • SECOND BATTLE

The Wetland Struggle


At the same time, rice farmers across Khamoto Parish were facing another battle - with the Mbale District Environment Office over farming in wetlands.


The government wanted to ban wetland farming to protect the environment. But for farmers in Khamoto, rice is their only livelihood. Through mediation, local leaders pushed for a middle way: regulated, eco-friendly farming committees instead of outright evictions.






PART 9 • TODAY & FUTURE

Today, Khamoto Stands


Today, Khamoto stands as a community that has survived land conflict, identity tensions, and environmental pressure - and is still standing.


"We want peace, education for our children, and a future for our farmers."
